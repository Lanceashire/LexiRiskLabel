\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Credit decisions determine which repayment outcomes become available for later model training. Approved applicants generate observed outcomes, whereas rejected applicants remain selectively unlabeled. This dependence can make both evaluation and retraining reflect the policy that collected the data rather than the population of applicants \cite{lakkaraju2017selective,kleinberg2018human}. In credit applications, obtaining feedback from a rejected region also requires accepting the associated predicted exposure. Feedback acquisition is therefore a resource-allocation problem: a selector chooses which outcomes to observe, how many to obtain, and how much predicted loss to spend.

Existing work provides tools for this setting. Reject inference corrects or augments samples affected by acceptance decisions \cite{banasik2007reject,marshall2010selection}; selective-label methods study sequential decisions with one-sided feedback \cite{wei2021selective,jiang2021onesided}; and exploration methods address self-confirming rejection, bounded debiasing, or controlled approvals \cite{pacchiano2021plot,yang2022bounded,scarone2026illusion}. Cost-sensitive active learning already treats sample costs as heterogeneous \cite{donmez2008proactive,tomanek2010cost}. These strands establish the problem and several useful selection scores, but final prediction metrics alone do not reveal the exchange among the number of labels obtained, the predicted risk spent, and the applicants selected.

We make that exchange explicit with lexicographic risk-budgeted feedback acquisition (LRBE). At a given policy state, let $K_t^*$ be the largest number of candidate labels that fits the predicted-loss budget, and let $M_t(k)$ be the minimum predicted risk of any feasible set of size $k$. A score can then be evaluated against two concrete references: the maximum feasible count and the minimum risk required to retain that count. The quantity loss $D(S)=K_t^*-|S|$ records labels exchanged for score; the composition premium $P(S)=\widehat R(S)-M_t(|S|)$ records extra predicted risk at the selected count. This construction is a comparison benchmark, not a claim that sorting costs is a new optimization algorithm.

The empirical study answers three questions. First, how many feasible labels does a score-first policy give up? Dynamic replay on HMEQ, LendingClub, UCI, and PPDai shows that LRBE-IG obtains 31.3, 49.5, and 40.1 more labels than ScoreFirst-IG on HMEQ, LendingClub, and UCI at $\rho=0.1$. Second, what risk premium accompanies a different composition at a similar cumulative count? Relative to MinRisk, LRBE-IG spends 28,765.5 additional predicted-risk units on HMEQ and 27,025.7 on LendingClub. Third, do these quantity and risk changes reach the prediction target? Recall and Brier differences vary in direction across datasets; UCI Brier error increases for the LRBE-IG versus ScoreFirst-IG contrast. Same-state audits confirm that IG changes selected sets, while the controlled experiment reproduces the immediate count--risk trade-off after one retraining step.

The paper makes three contributions. (1) It provides a measurable comparison benchmark based on the maximum feasible count $K^*$ and the same-count minimum-risk profile $M(k)$, making the quantity opportunity cost and composition premium of an acquisition score visible. (2) It combines dynamic replay, same-state selection comparison, and controlled one-step retraining so that policy evolution, immediate selector behavior, and predictive outcomes are reported as distinct evidence blocks. (3) It gives an empirical finding across four credit datasets: score choice changes feedback quantity and predicted-risk use more consistently than it changes downstream prediction, whose direction is dataset-dependent. The resulting framework clarifies what a higher acquisition score buys and what it costs.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Selective labels and controlled exploration.}
Selective-label research studies learning when decisions determine observed outcomes \cite{lakkaraju2017selective,kleinberg2018human}. Expert consistency, fair decisions, and one-sided-feedback learning extend reasoning beyond existing acceptance patterns \cite{dearteaga2018expert,kilbertus2020fair,jiang2021onesided}. Reject inference and sample-selection correction address credit-specific observation bias \cite{banasik2007reject,marshall2010selection}. Our replay uses observed cohort outcomes and reveals them under simulated approvals, so the estimand is policy behavior under replay.

\paragraph{Cost-sensitive and batch acquisition.}
Cost-sensitive active learning studies heterogeneous annotation costs and budgeted sample selection \cite{donmez2008proactive,tomanek2010cost}. ConBatch-BAL develops Bayesian batch acquisition under budget constraints, including dynamic thresholding and greedy selection based on uncertainty scores \cite{morato2025conbatchbal}. Batch-level uncertainty methods therefore provide a close reference for allocating a finite acquisition budget. Our comparison asks a narrower question in selective credit feedback: after the current maximum feasible count and the same-count minimum-risk profile are fixed, how much quantity and predicted-risk change is associated with a different composition score?

\paragraph{Exploration, safety, and evaluation.}
PLOT uses optimistic pseudo-labels to counter self-confirming rejection, while adaptive bounded exploration trades debiasing against accepting otherwise rejected individuals \cite{pacchiano2021plot,yang2022bounded}. Partial-feedback exploration supports subgroup coverage and fairness objectives \cite{keswani2024partial}; cost-efficient fairness auditing accounts for assessment, processing, and potential-default costs \cite{das2025cost}; and controlled approvals in credit scoring examine retraining under selection bias \cite{scarone2026illusion}. These works supply complementary objectives and safeguards. LRBE uses a point predicted-loss allowance as a ledger, then reports the resulting quantity, composition, predicted-risk, and downstream changes together. The benchmark makes the cost of a score visible even when the score is useful for selecting a different set of applicants.
