\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a quantity-preserving reference contributes.}
Maximizing feasible count makes the opportunity cost of a secondary acquisition score observable. Removing the count equality with FAVE, IG, or random-order selection reduces feedback substantially on the three datasets with informative count ranges. The resulting predictive estimates vary by dataset: HMEQ Recall favors LRBE-FAVE, UCI Brier favors QualityFirst-IG, and the remaining primary intervals span both directions. More feedback is therefore an intermediate outcome whose value must be checked against prediction. Within the eligibility rule and predicted-risk constraint, MinRisk states the least predicted risk required to retain the current maximum count.

\paragraph{Policy effects and composition evidence.}
The three experimental views answer different questions. Dynamic replay compares complete acquisition policies that begin with the same data stream and then evolve through policy-specific feedback, predictions, candidates, and budgets. Same-state audits fix those quantities and show how selectors exchange count, risk, and score at one decision point. The controlled experiment adds one retraining step after fixing the acquisition state. It reproduces the count and predicted-risk contrasts, while its exposure and target-weight interactions remain uncertain. This division connects long-run policy outcomes to an immediate selection mechanism without treating the two analyses as interchangeable.

\paragraph{Why predictive differences can remain small.}
Most labels added in the controlled task come from ordinary approvals shared across selectors: about 3,751 per incoming batch, compared with exploratory $K^*$ means of 29.55 under unit exposure and 118.7 under heterogeneous exposure. Differences among exploratory selections therefore modify only a fraction of the post-acquisition training set. This scale contrast offers one plausible account of the small Brier differences. The experiment varies exposure and target weight, leaving dilution as a possible explanation for subsequent study. More broadly, an acquisition score matters to prediction when the labels it changes affect the fitted model and target metric; score totals and feedback composition describe earlier links in that chain.

\paragraph{Scope of inference.}
The evidence concerns random-order replay on historical cohorts with observed outcomes. LendingClub contains observed loan outcomes rather than outcomes for actual historical rejects, labels arrive immediately in the simulation, and $\tau=0.5$ supplies a common experimental operating point. PPDai contributes unit-exposure exploratory evidence; HMEQ and LendingClub supply monetary heterogeneity. The registered IG comparison uses $\rho=0.1$, while the broader budget sweep covers the original policy set. Published online exploration methods motivate the research question, and the randomized controls isolate design choices within the present replay. The conclusions concern these acquisition policies and replay conditions.

\paragraph{Risk interpretation.}
An optimizer can enforce a sum of predicted losses while realized loss remains variable. Here, an exceedance means that realized defaulted exposure in a seed--round observation is larger than the policy's point-estimate budget. This ex-post comparison with expected loss combines probability error, default randomness, and exposure assumptions. A live credit experiment would additionally specify product-level exposure and recovery, delayed outcomes, concentration and fairness safeguards, and stopping rules. The current point-cost constraint supplies a planning ledger for the replay rather than those operational controls.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Risk-budgeted feedback acquisition involves two choices: how many outcomes to make observable and which applicants should provide them. LRBE separates them by preserving the current maximum feasible count before applying a composition score. Across FAVE, committee IG, and randomized controls, removing the count constraint often reduces feedback substantially. Preserving those labels produces dataset-specific predictive changes rather than uniform gains. At similar cumulative counts, FAVE and IG also use more predicted risk than MinRisk, while their Recall and Brier differences remain small and uncertain in the primary comparisons. The controlled analysis reproduces the selection trade-offs, with unresolved exposure and target-weight interactions. Evaluating quantity, predicted-risk use, composition, and downstream performance together shows where an acquisition score changes the learning process and whether that change reaches the prediction target.
